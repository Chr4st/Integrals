\begin{abstract}
We present a hybrid system for symbolic integration that tries SymPy first, then falls back to a 44.8M-parameter Transformer whose outputs are verified by differentiation. Since differentiation is cheap and exact, we can sample 25 diverse candidates and accept the first one that checks out, giving a success probability of $1 - (1 - p)^N$. We also introduce depth-encoded structural features, grammar-constrained decoding, and a two-phase constant solver. The full pipeline reaches 84.7\% solve rate on our test set, up from 68.7\% with SymPy alone, and every returned result is guaranteed correct.
\end{abstract}

symbolic integration, transformer, verification oracle, constrained decoding, computer algebra
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Symbolic integration is about finding a closed-form antiderivative $F(x)$ such that $dF/dx = f(x)$. Computer algebra systems like SymPy and Mathematica use variants of the Risch algorithm \cite{b28} and pattern matching to handle a wide range of integrals, but they regularly choke on deeply nested compositions and expressions outside their rule databases \cite{b24}.

Neural approaches, starting with Lample and Charton \cite{b19}, treat integration as sequence-to-sequence translation: serialize the integrand in prefix notation, have a Transformer generate the antiderivative token by token. This works surprisingly well (over 95\% on their benchmarks), but the outputs are unverified. A predicted antiderivative might look right but be mathematically wrong.

The thing that makes integration special is that there is a perfect verification oracle. Differentiation is fast, always terminates, and is exact. Given a candidate $\hat{F}(x)$, you just check whether $\frac{d}{dx}\hat{F}(x) = f(x)$. This is fundamentally different from majority-voting approaches like self-consistency \cite{b33} where the verifier is approximate. With differentiation, a single correct candidate out of $N$ is enough. If each sample is correct with probability $p$, then $N$ independent samples give you $P(\text{success}) = 1 - (1 - p)^N$. Even with $p = 0.6$ and $N = 25$, that exceeds 99.99\%. So instead of trying to make each individual prediction better, we just generate a bunch of diverse candidates and verify them.

Our system has four main contributions. First, we build a two-stage pipeline where SymPy gets the first shot (4s timeout), and the Transformer only runs if SymPy fails. We use temperature sampling ($N = 25$, $T = 0.7$, top-$p = 0.95$) as the primary inference strategy. Second, we introduce 608-dimensional depth-encoded structural features ($76$ function heads $\times$ 8 logarithmic depth bins) with a three-way power classification that distinguishes polynomial power ($x^2$), exponential ($2^x$), and tower ($x^x$) forms. These get injected into the encoder as a learned \texttt{[FEAT]} token. Third, we implement grammar-constrained decoding via prefix-notation arity stack tracking, which prevents the model from generating syntactically invalid sequences and wastes fewer sample slots. Fourth, we have a two-phase constant resolution pipeline that first tries symbolic solve (3s), then falls back to Sobol quasi-random numeric optimization with L-BFGS-B and cascading rationalization.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~II covers how we represent expressions as token sequences. Section~III describes data generation. Section~IV presents the method. Section~V has experimental results. Section~VI discusses limitations and analysis. Section~VII concludes.

\section{Expressions as Sequences}

We use the prefix (Polish) notation encoding from Lample and Charton \cite{b19}, where expressions are serialized as token sequences from their abstract syntax trees.

\subsection{Prefix Notation}

An expression tree has operators at internal nodes and operands at leaves. We do a pre-order traversal: operators come before their arguments. Binary operators (Add, Mul, Pow) take two arguments, unary ones (sin, cos, exp, log) take one. N-ary operators get serialized as nested binary operations, so $\text{Add}(a, b, c)$ becomes \texttt{[Add, a, Add, b, c]}. This encoding is lossless and unambiguous without needing parentheses.

\subsection{Base-100 Integer Tokenization}

We use a compact integer representation where integers are encoded in base-100 with a sign prefix:

Decoding is just $\text{val} = \text{val} \times 100 + \text{chunk}$. This replaces the digit-by-digit encoding from \cite{b19} where 123456 would need 7 tokens. Rationals are encoded as \texttt{[Rational, INT+\_p, INT+\_q]} where $p/q$ is in lowest terms.

\subsection{Vocabulary}

The sequence vocabulary has 204 tokens: 4 special tokens (PAD, BOS, EOS, UNK), 2 sign markers, 20 constant placeholders ($C_1$ through $C_{20}$), 78 operators and functions (trig, hyperbolic, special functions, structural operators, symbols), and 100 base-100 digit tokens. The feature vocabulary defines 76 function heads for structural feature extraction (Section~IV-B).

\section{Generating Training Data}

Following \cite{b19}, we primarily use backward generation to produce verified integrand-antiderivative pairs. We supplement this with a curated dataset and augmentation.

\textbf{Backward generation.} We generate random expression trees $F(x)$ at configurable depth, differentiate to get $f(x) = dF/dx$, and since differentiation always works, we get verified pairs for free. We have three difficulty tiers: easy (depth 1-2), medium (3-4), and hard (5-6). The operator set covers trig, hyperbolic, exp, log, sqrt, and basic arithmetic. Domain guards keep things well-defined: logarithms wrap their argument in $|\text{child}| + 1$, square roots compose with $\text{child}^2 + 1$, and inverse trig functions get bounded inputs. Coefficients come from integers in $[-10, 10] \setminus \{0\}$ and simple rationals. Everything gets simplified and deduplicated by hash.

\textbf{SIRD dataset processing.} We also process the SIRD dataset with about 2.1 million integrand records. Each one gets integrated with SymPy (10s timeout), verified by differentiation, and filtered for residual \texttt{Integral} or \texttt{Piecewise} terms. After tokenization and length filtering (input $\leq$ 382, output $\leq$ 254 tokens), roughly 243,000 verified pairs survive, about an 11.6\% yield.

\textbf{Expression augmentation.} We apply three augmentation strategies that preserve mathematical equivalence: commutativity permutation, expand/factor rewriting, and trigonometric rewriting. All variants share the same target antiderivative, giving us a 3-4$\times$ effective dataset multiplier.

\section{Method}

\subsection{System Overview}

The system is a two-stage pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Stage~1 tries SymPy with a 4-second timeout. If it works and the result is clean (no leftover \texttt{Integral} or \texttt{Piecewise} terms), we return it. If not, Stage~2 kicks in with the Transformer (15s timeout). The ML stage tokenizes the integrand, extracts structural features, generates candidates, and verifies each one by differentiation. Worst case end-to-end latency is 19 seconds.

SymPy runs in a persistent \texttt{ProcessPoolExecutor} so we can reliably kill it on timeout. The ML stage uses a \texttt{ThreadPoolExecutor} to share GPU memory.

\subsection{Depth-Encoded Structural Features}

Prefix sequences lose depth information. A shallow expression like $\sin(x) + \cos(x)$ and a deeply nested one like $\sin(\cos(\exp(\log(x))))$ look similar at the token level but need completely different integration strategies.

We fix this with a 608-dimensional feature vector computed by walking the SymPy expression tree depth-first and recording which function heads show up at which nesting depths.

\textbf{Feature vocabulary.} We define 76 function heads covering trig (12), inverse trig (12), hyperbolic (12), inverse hyperbolic (12), exp and log (5), special functions, structural operators, and numeric types. The key innovation is a three-way power classification: \textbf{Pow} means the variable is only in the base (like $x^2$), \textbf{Exp} means it is only in the exponent (like $2^x$), and \textbf{Tower} means it is in both (like $x^x$). These three cases need fundamentally different integration approaches.

\textbf{Logarithmic depth binning.} Instead of recording exact depths, we bin into 8 logarithmic buckets with boundaries at $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 15\}$. Depths 1-4 get individual bins; deeper levels are grouped.

\textbf{Injection.} The feature vector goes through a learned linear layer ($608 \rightarrow 512$) and gets prepended to the encoder input as a \texttt{[FEAT]} token. We also store a SHA-256 hash of the feature schema in each checkpoint so we can detect incompatible changes at load time.

\subsection{Model Architecture}

We use a pre-norm encoder-decoder Transformer \cite{b34} with sinusoidal positional encodings \cite{b32} and Xavier uniform initialization.

\textbf{Encoder.} Input tokens are embedded and scaled by $\sqrt{d_\text{model}}$. The 608-dim feature vector gets projected to $d_\text{model}$ and prepended as a \texttt{[FEAT]} token. Then sinusoidal positional encodings and 6 pre-norm encoder layers with self-attention.

\textbf{Decoder.} Target tokens are embedded, scaled, and positionally encoded. Six pre-norm decoder layers apply causal self-attention and cross-attention to encoder memory. A final linear projection gives vocabulary logits.

\textbf{Training.} We use Adam ($\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.98$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$) with gradient clipping (max\_norm = 1.0). Learning rate follows warmup-then-inverse-square-root:
\begin{equation}
\text{lr}(t) = \text{lr}_\text{base} \cdot \min\!\left(\frac{t}{t_\text{warmup}},\; \sqrt{\frac{t_\text{warmup}}{t}}\right)
\end{equation}
with $\text{lr}_\text{base} = 4 \times 10^{-5}$ and $t_\text{warmup} = 4{,}000$ steps. Loss is cross-entropy with inverse-frequency class weights (capped at 10.0), ignoring PAD tokens. We train up to 50 epochs with early stopping (patience 5) on exact match. Following Izmailov et al. \cite{b17}, we average the last $K = 5$ epoch checkpoints (SWA), which gives a consistent +1-2\% accuracy boost for free.

\subsection{Sampling with a Verification Oracle}

The whole inference strategy is built around the fact that integration is hard but differentiation is easy. Given a candidate $\hat{F}(x)$, we just check if $\text{simplify}(\frac{d}{dx}\hat{F}(x) - f(x)) = 0$. This is fast, deterministic, and exact. With a perfect verifier, any correct candidate among $N$ samples is enough:
\begin{equation}
P(\text{pipeline success}) = 1 - (1 - p)^N
\label{eq:sampling}
\end{equation}

This is not like majority voting where you need most samples to agree. Here, one correct sample out of 25 is sufficient.

\textbf{Primary strategy: temperature sampling.} We generate 25 candidates with temperature $T = 0.7$ and nucleus filtering (top-$p = 0.95$) \cite{b16}, batched in groups of 5. Each candidate is verified immediately; the first one that passes gets returned. If a candidate has template constants ($C_1, C_2, \ldots$), it goes through the constant solver first (Section~IV-F).

\textbf{Fallback: beam search.} If none of the 25 samples verify, we try beam search (width 10) with length penalty \cite{b35}: $\text{score}(y) = \log P(y) / ((5 + |y|) / 6)^\alpha$, $\alpha = 0.7$.

\subsection{Grammar-Constrained Decoding}

Unconstrained decoding can produce syntactically invalid prefix sequences, like a binary operator followed by EOS before its second argument. These waste sample slots.

We implement a \texttt{PrefixGrammarMask} that tracks the arity stack during generation and masks out invalid next tokens. Binary operators (Add, Mul, Pow) increment the ``need'' counter by 1. Unary operators (sin, cos, exp) leave it unchanged. Terminals ($x$, $\pi$, constants) decrement it by 1. Integer sign tokens (INT+/INT$-$) enter a special mode where at least one digit must follow. EOS is only valid when need equals 0. The mask sets invalid logits to $-\infty$ before softmax.

\subsection{Two-Phase Constant Resolution}

The Transformer sometimes predicts templates with placeholder constants $C_1, C_2, \ldots$ that need to be resolved. For example, given integrand $\sin(x)$, it might predict $C_1 \cdot \cos(x) + C_2$.

Phase~1 is symbolic solve with a 3-second timeout. We differentiate the template and pass $dT/dx = f(x)$ to SymPy's \texttt{solve()}.

Phase~2 kicks in when symbolic solve fails. We generate evaluation points on $[-10, 10]$ using scrambled Sobol sequences, split them 70/30 into train and verify sets, and run multi-start L-BFGS-B (3 restarts) to minimize the MSE between $dT/dx$ and $f(x)$. A solution is accepted if train MSE $\leq 10^{-6}$ and verification max error $\leq 10^{-4}$. Then we rationalize each numeric constant using \texttt{nsimplify()} with hints ($\pi$, $e$, $\sqrt{2}$, $\sqrt{3}$, $\log 2$, $\varphi$) at tolerances $[10^{-10}, 10^{-8}, 10^{-6}]$. The final filled template is verified by differentiation.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

\textbf{Data.} We train on backward-generated pairs combined with processed SIRD pairs. The test set is a 10\% stratified holdout. We evaluate per-difficulty-tier and overall.

\textbf{Metrics.} We report solve rate (fraction returning a verified result), token accuracy (per-token under teacher forcing), exact match (greedy decoding matches ground truth), and latency.

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare four configurations: SymPy-only with 60s timeout, Transformer + beam search (width 10), Transformer + sampling ($N = 25$), and the full two-stage pipeline. Neural methods report mean $\pm$ std over 5 seeds.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}

\subsection{Error Analysis}

Failures fall into four categories. SymPy timeout: the integrand is too gnarly for the CAS in 4 seconds. No verified sample: we generate 25 temperature samples and 10 beam candidates but none pass the differentiation check. Constant solver failure: we get the right template structure but cannot resolve the constants in 13 seconds. Tokenization overflow: the integrand or antiderivative is too long for our 382/254 token limits.

\subsection{Limitations}

The system only handles single-variable indefinite integrals. No multivariate or definite integrals. Verification depends on SymPy being able to simplify $\frac{d}{dx}\hat{F}(x) - f(x)$ to zero, which sometimes fails even when the answer is correct (false negatives, but never false positives). The 384/256 token limits exclude very long expressions. And at 44.8M parameters the model is on the small side; scaling behavior \cite{b26} remains unexplored.

\subsection{Broader Applicability}

The verify-then-diversify idea works anywhere you have a hard generation task with a cheap exact verifier. That covers symbolic equation solving, identity verification, and formal theorem proving. The main takeaway is: when you have a perfect oracle, invest compute in candidate diversity rather than per-candidate quality. This flips the usual beam search thinking on its head.

\section{Conclusion}

We built a hybrid CAS-Transformer system for symbolic integration where every returned result is guaranteed correct by differentiation. The key idea is that with a perfect verifier, you want diverse candidates rather than individually better ones. Sampling 25 times with $T = 0.7$ works better than beam search because any single correct candidate is enough. The supporting pieces (depth features, grammar masking, constant solving) each contribute meaningfully, as shown in the ablation.

Future work includes tree-aware positional encodings \cite{b30b}, asymmetric encoder-decoder architectures \cite{b8}, self-training with the verification loop \cite{b25}, and scaling the model up \cite{b26}.
